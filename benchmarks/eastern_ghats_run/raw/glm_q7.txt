    WHAT THE ISOTOPES WOULD INDICATE (ecological framework, not measured values)
    
    δ13C (carbon) tracks the plant-base of the food web. In your landscape:
    - EBTL restoration site: dry-deciduous trees and shrubs are C3 plants (δ13C ≈ -27‰). A snake food web rooted in C3 plants — forest rodents, forest frogs, forest invertebrates — carries that depleted C3 signal up the chain. So shed skins / muscle from cobras and rat snakes foraging inside the restoration site should show δ13C values in the C3-terrestrial range (roughly -25 to -20‰ after trophic fractionation, which is small for carbon, ~+1‰ per level).
    - Intensively farmed landscape outside: the cropland belt (47.4 km², ESA WorldCover class 40) is dominated by C4 crops in this dry-deccan zone — maize, sorghum, pearl millet, finger millet (C4, δ13C ≈ -13‰) — plus some C3 paddy in the wetter pockets. Rodents feeding on C4 grain (Bandicota, Rattus in fields) carry a enriched δ13C signal. A snake eating those rodents would show δ13C shifted several per mil more positive (enriched) compared to a snake foraging inside EBTL. So the prediction: farmed-landscape snakes should be isotopically heavier (less negative δ13C) than restoration-site snakes, and the magnitude of that shift tells you how much of the prey base is C4-crop-derived vs C3-forest-derived.
    
    δ15N (nitrogen) tracks trophic level — it enriches by ~+3 to +5‰ per trophic step.
    - Both species are mid-predators. The Indian cobra (Naja naja) is a rodent-and-frog specialist that also takes other snakes, lizards, and occasionally invertebrates; the rat snake (Ptyas mucosa) is primarily a rodent specialist but also takes frogs, lizards, birds, and eggs. If cobras are feeding one trophic step above the rat snake (e.g., taking other snake or lizard predators that themselves feed on rodents), you'd expect cobra δ15N to run ~3-5‰ higher than rat snake δ15N at the same site. If they're at the same trophic level (both eating rodents directly), their δ15N should be similar.
    - Prey-base discrimination within a species: a cobra or rat snake whose δ15N is low relative to its peers, with a C3 δ13C, is likely drawing prey from the forest-floor invertebrate → frog/ lizard chain (shorter chain, lower baseline δ15N in a dry forest). One whose δ15N is high with a C4 δ13C is likely drawing prey from the crop-rodent chain (agricultural rodents often sit on a higher δ15N baseline because of synthetic fertiliser — urea/ammonium nitrate — which elevates soil and plant δ15N, though synthetic fertiliser can actually depress δ15N toward 0‰ compared to manure-amended fields). This is where it gets interesting and site-specific: the fertiliser regime of the surrounding cropland will set the δ15N baseline of the rodent prey, and the snake tissue will record it.
    - Rodents vs frogs vs invertebrates as prey: you can't fully separate these three from snake tissue alone with just δ13C and δ15N — you need the prey isotope baseline (rodent muscle, frog muscle, invertebrate samples from the same plots) to build a mixing model (SIAR/SIMMR). Without prey baselines, δ13C/δ15N in the snake tells you "terrestrial C3 vs agricultural C4" and "relative trophic level" but not which of the three prey categories dominates. Frogs and invertebrates from a C3 forest floor will look similar in δ13C to forest rodents; the δ15N step between invertebrate → frog → snake vs rodent → snake is what separates them, and that requires the baseline.

Don't compare raw shed-skin values against raw muscle values without a tissue-discrimination factor.
