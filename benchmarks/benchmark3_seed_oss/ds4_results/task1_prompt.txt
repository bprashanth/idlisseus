Forest Fire in Himalayan Regions: An analysis
Bhagawati Prashad Sharma, Niraj Kumar, Sanjeet Kumar and Rajkumari Supriya
Devi*
Abstract: Forest fires in the Himalayan region have devastating consequences,
impacting ecosystems, economy, and local communities. The region's diverse forests,
including Chir Pine, Broad-leaved deciduous, and Himalayan moist temperate forests
are prone to fires due to factors like dry pine needles, leaf litter, and human activity.
Human-induced fires, whether intentional or accidental, are the primary cause of
forest fires in the region. The consequences of these fires are severe, with significant
damage to the environment, wildlife, and local communities. In Uttarakhand, forest
fires affected large forest areas in May 2019. Forest fires also lead to significant
carbon emissions, loss of biodiversity, soil erosion, and changes in ecosystem
structure and function. Rising temperatures and global warming are expected to
increase forest fires in the region. Present study aims to provide a comprehensive
review of forest fires in the Himalayan ranges, examining their characteristics,
underlying causes, impacts on vegetation, and the role of community involvement in
prevention and management efforts. Effective forest fire management strategies are
essential to mitigate these impacts and protect the region's rich biodiversity and
ecosystem services.

INTRODUCTION
The Himalayan Mountain range stretches across the northern edge of the Indian
subcontinent, featuring a complex series of mountain ranges that ascend from the
southern Indo-Gangetic plains to the northern Tibetan plateau. This vast region
encompasses various ecosystems, landscapes, and landforms, largely due to
significant variations in precipitation patterns (Owen, 2014). The Western Himalayas
are characterized by diverse forest types, including alpine, semi-evergreen,
deciduous, and sub-tropical forests. These forests are found in states such as Jammu
and Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, and parts of Punjab and Haryana,
where coniferous Chir pine forests dominate (Bhatt et al., 2022). Forest fires are a
pressing concern in this region, particularly in areas with dry Chir pine needles and
leaf litter. The frequency and intensity of fires depend on factors like forest type, fuel
load, and microclimatic conditions. Deciduous forests are especially vulnerable to
fires, accounting for approximately 40% of India's forest fires. The Southern and
Western parts of the Himalayan region are more prone to forest fires due to the
presence of dry and mixed forest types (Bhatt et al., 2022). In 2016, forest fires
affected over 4,500 hectares in Himachal Pradesh and 3,185 hectares in Uttarakhand,
resulting in significant ecological, economic, and social impacts (Dobriyal and
Bijalwan, 2017). Forest fires are often intentional, set for purposes like resource
collection or grass growth. The Chir pine's dominance and the ban on green felling
above 1,000 meters elevation have contributed to the spread of forest fires. The
needles of Chir pine are used for various purposes, but their limited utilization has
led to a buildup of highly flammable material. The migration of villagers from the
hilly state has reduced local utilization of pine needles, exacerbating the issue. The
inaccessibility of forest areas and low rainfall with dry spells in winter further
complicate efforts to combat forest fires (Dobriyal and Bijalwan, 2017).
Causes of Forest Fires in Himalayan Region
Forest fires can be categorized into three main types: natural, man-made, and
accidental. The Chir Pine tree, with its resin-rich needle leaves and twigs, is highly
susceptible to forest fires. In Uttarakhand, the annual shedding of pine needles creates
a significant fire hazard, particularly during the summer months when temperatures
rise (Negi, 2019). Human activity is the primary cause of forest fires in the region,
with intentional and accidental ignitions being the main contributors. Intentional fires
are often set to clear land, promote new growth, or extract resources, while accidental
fires can start from escaped agricultural burns, discarded cigarettes, or other human
activities (Bargali et al., 2022). Natural fires, on the other hand, are rare and typically
caused by lightening or other environmental factors (Bhattarai et al., 2022). However,
the combination of drought, climate change, and human activity has created a perfect

storm for forest fires in Uttarakhand. The main causes of forest fires in Uttarakhand
are diverse and multifaceted. Human activities such as jhumming/shifting cultivation,
burning agricultural biomass waste, and throwing cigarette butts and biddies without
properly extinguishing them are major contributors (Arya et al., 2022). Accidental
fires caused by campfires, sparks from vehicles, and electricity also play a significant
role. Additionally, intentional fires set to drive away wild animals, collect non-timber
forest products, or conceal illicit felling are other major causes of forest fires in the
region (Bhatt et al., 2022). The region's dominance of fire-prone tree species like Chir
Pine also contributes to the frequent occurrence of forest fires. States like
Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh, and Sikkim are among the most affected by forest
fires in India (Joshi and Sharma, 2015). The consequences of these fires can be
devastating, with significant damage to the environment, wildlife, and local
communities. Some notable incidents of forest fires in the region highlight the
severity of the issue. In 1995, around 3.75 lakh hectares of forest were destroyed in
the Ganga-Yamuna junction region of Uttarakhand. Similarly, in 1999, around 80,000
hectares of forest were affected in Uttarakhand. Again, in 2010, around 19,000
hectares of forests were affected by fire in Himachal Pradesh (Murmu et al., 2022).
Impact of Forest fires on the Himalayan region
Forest fires in the Himalayan region have severe and far-reaching consequences,
affecting the ecosystem, economy, and livelihoods of local communities, as well as
posing significant threats to biodiversity, natural resources, and regional stabilityF.
The region's rich biodiversity is under threat from frequent fires, which fragment
ecosystems, alter their structure and function, and decimate plant and animal life
(Mina et al., 2023). The density and diversity of forest ecosystems are severely
impacted, particularly in the mountainous regions where fires are less frequent. In
Nepal, forest fire events have increased in recent years, primarily during the dry
season from November to June, altering plant composition, diversity, and
regeneration, and resulting in reduced floral productivity, altered regeneration rates,
and loss of endemic and endangered species. Bare soil becomes vulnerable to erosion
after fires, especially in areas with no surface cover. The effect of forest fires varies
across Nepal's ecological zones, including the Terai, Mid-hill, and Mountain regions.
In Nepal, forest fires have caused significant damage, with approximately 172,040.65
hectares of forest land affected between 2001 and 2020, resulting in a biomass loss
of around 7.07 million tons (Bhujel et al., 2022; Joshi et al., 2024). The Tarai and
Siwalik regions experienced the largest scale forest damage, followed by the Midhills, High Mountain, and Himalaya regions.

In the Himalaya region of Uttarakhand, anthropogenic activities, such as accidental
and intentional fires, contribute to forest fires, which are expected to increase due to
rising temperatures and global warming (Bargali et al., 2022). Chir pine (Pinus
roxburghii) forests dominate approximately 16.36% of the forest area in the region
and are highly prone to fires, with the accumulation of Chir pine needles on the forest
floor leading to large-scale destruction of forests in hilly areas (Dobriyal and Bijalwan
2017; Bhatt et al., 2022). Furthermore, forest fires release large volumes of smoke,

health, with the impact on human health being significant, and increased risk of
respiratory problems and other health issues (Murmu et al., 2022). Additionally,
frequent fires and uncontrolled grazing have depleted biodiversity in areas like TehriGarhwal Himalayas, exacerbating the ecological impacts of forest fires.
Impact of Forest fires on the Vegetation
The forest vegetation in the Himalayan region varies across different states, including
Jammu & Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh, and Uttarakhand. In Jammu & Kashmir, the
forests consist of Chir Pine, broad-leaved deciduous, broad-leaved evergreen, and
scrub forest, interspaced with grassland patches, and feature species like Quercus
leucotrichophora, Pinus roxburghii, and Cedrus deodara (Bhatt et al., 2022; Malik
et al., 2025). In Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand, the forests include Tropical
Thorn, Tropical semi-evergreen, and Himalayan moist temperate forests, with species
like Pinus spp., Deodar, Kail, Fir, and Spruce. Pinus spp. (Figure 1) is highly
inflammable due to its high resin content, making it prone to forest fires (Figures 23). Deciduous forests are also highly susceptible to fires, accounting for around 40%
of all forest fires in India. Forest fires have a significant impact on these regions
(Kumari et al., 2025). In Uttarakhand, forest fires affected an average of 644 hectares
each, resulting in a total loss of 10.53 crore Indian rupees in May 2019. The increasing
frequency of forest fires in the region may hinder the achievement of India's
Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) plan goals (Mina et al., 2023). The fires
have also led to loss of biodiversity, soil erosion, and changes in ecosystem structure
and function. In the Sikkim Himalayas, the alpine vegetation in the region comprises
of various scrub and meadow types, including Juniperus indica scrub, Rhododendron
scrub, Kobresia duthiei moist meadows, Kobresia nepalensis moist meadows,
Kobresia pygmaea dry meadows, and Anaphalis xylorhiza mixed meadows,
showcasing the region's diverse and adapted flora. However, the region's unique and
fragile ecosystems are vulnerable to forest fires, which can have long-term
consequences for the environment and local communities. Overall, the Himalayan
region's diverse forest ecosystems are vulnerable to forest fires, which can have
significant ecological, economic, and social impacts (Tambe and Rawat, 2010).
CONCLUSION
The Himalayan region is highly susceptible to forest fires, which have severe
ecological, economic, and social impacts. Human activities, such as intentional and
accidental fires, are the primary cause of forest fires in the region, with factors like
climate change, drought, and the dominance of fire-prone tree species like Chir Pine
exacerbating the issue. Forest fires in the region have resulted in significant damage
to the environment, wildlife, and local communities, with notable incidents
