CHAPTER 8
Models of Forest Fire Management in India
Anuradha Das, Rajkumari Supriya Devi and Sanjeet Kumar
Abstract: Forest fires are a significant threat to India's forest ecosystems, biodiversity, and local
communities. To mitigate and manage forest fires, the Indian government has launched various
initiatives and policies. The National Forest Fire Action Plan published in 2013, aims to prevent, detect,
and control forest fires. Additionally, various states have their own policies and guidelines for forest
fire management. India also collaborates with international organizations to share knowledge and
resources. Effective forest fire management involves prevention, detection, control, and community
engagement. Prevention measures include creating fire breaks and conducting awareness campaigns.
Detection involves using satellite imagery, drones, and ground-based sensors to detect forest fires early.
Control measures include suppressing forest fires using techniques like manual beating, smothering,
counter-firing, fire lines, and dousing. Community engagement is also crucial, as local communities
can be educated about forest fire risks and involved in prevention and detection efforts. Furthermore,
post-fire management is essential, involving assessing damage, rehabilitating affected areas, and
restoring ecosystems. Various forest fire management models, such as the "Prescribed Burning Model",
"Fire Suppression Model", and "Community-based Fire Management Model" can be adopted and
tailored to suit specific regional conditions. The study aims to document the different forest models
persisting in India. These models can help optimize forest fire management strategies, reduce the risk
of forest fires, and promote sustainable forest management practices. To improve forest fire
management capacity building, community participation, technology integration, and a comprehensive
policy framework are necessary.

INTRODUCTION
Forest fires have been an inherent aspect of forest ecosystems for centuries.
However, the escalating environmental challenges, including rising temperatures,

dwindling precipitation, intense dry winds, and plummeting humidity levels have
profoundly impacted the delicate balance of forest ecosystems. In response to the
devastating consequences of forest fires, including the loss of biodiversity and
depletion of forest resources, the Government of India has launched a plethora of
initiatives and policies aimed at mitigating and managing forest fires across the
country (Bargali et al., 2014). These efforts underscore the government's
commitment to preserving India's rich forest heritage and ensuring the long-term
sustainability of its ecosystems. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO)
implemented a specialized Technical Cooperation Programme (TCP) project in
India, focusing on human resource development in forest fire management.
Launched in May 1995 and completed in December 1995, the project aimed to
enhance India's capacity in forest fire management by assessing the country's forest
fire situation, providing training in strategic fire planning, and developing a model
State Forest Fire Management Plan (FSI, 2019a). The project's recommendations,
provided by an international consultant team, emphasized the need for a preventive
program, coordination between government agencies, a national awareness
campaign, a central fire management research and training institute, fire experiment
stations, and integration of fire management into the Joint Forest Management
framework (FSI, 2019b). Between 2000 and 2009, most Indian states,
approximately 58% were vulnerable to severe forest fires. Of these 34% experienced
extremely severe fires, while 13% and 10% were affected by very high and highintensity fires respectively. The states most prone to extreme forest fires during this
period included Andhra Pradesh, Assam, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, Maharashtra,
Madhya Pradesh, Manipur, Mizoram, and Nagaland (Mohanty and Mithal, 2022).
From a governance perspective, forest fires fall under the jurisdiction of the Ministry
of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC) and the State Forest
Departments (SFDs) at the state level. The management of forest fires is overseen
by the National Afforestation Programme (NAP) and the Forest Fire Prevention and
Management Scheme. In 2018, the MoEFCC introduced the National Action Plan
on Forest Fires (NAPFF), which seeks to engage and empower local communities
in reducing forest fire incidents. However, the NAPFF still awaits dedicated funding
and relies on allocations from the Compensatory Afforestation Fund Management
and Planning Authority (Mohanty and Mithal, 2022; ISFR, 2023).
India's constitution empowers both Central and State Governments to legislate on
forestry issues, but the onus of implementing policies and programs lies solely with
the State Governments. The responsibility of preventing, detecting, and suppressing
forest fires falls on the State Forest Department, which often lacks a dedicated unit
or wing for this critical task. As a result, regular staff must take on this responsibility

without additional support or resources, and in many cases, without a dedicated fund
for fire management activities. To address this challenge, the Union Ministry of
Environment and Forests launched the "Modern Forest Fire Control Project" in
1984, with assistance from the United Nations Development Program, in Uttar
Pradesh and Maharashtra. The project's success led to the introduction of a centrally
sponsored scheme, "Modern Forest Fire Control Methods," in 11 states in 1992-93,
which was later extended to all states and union territories in 2000. The scheme aims
to achieve several key objectives, including controlling forest fires to protect and
conserve forests, as well as developing, testing, and demonstrating effective forest
fire management principles and techniques. Additionally, the scheme seeks to
enhance forest productivity by reducing the incidence and extent of forest fires.
Raising public awareness about the detrimental effects of forest fires on forests and
the environment is also a crucial objective (PCWFFMI, 2022). Furthermore, the
scheme aims to empower forest officials and local communities through training
programs, equipping them with the skills and knowledge necessary to prevent,
detect, and control forest fires. Some of the common equipments used for the
management of forest fire include beaters, leaf blower, lightweight fire
extinguishers, fire resistant uniforms, helmets, transport vehicles and
communication devices, etc (Figure 1). The research activities on forest fire initially
started around 1982 and rise to manifold in the present day.
The management of forest fires involves a multi-faceted approach that encompasses
a series of strategic steps (Kumar et al., 2023). It begins with prevention activities,
including creating firebreaks, conducting prescribed burns, educating public, and
enforcing regulations (Reddy et al., 2017). Detection is the next crucial step, which
involves monitoring weather conditions, using surveillance systems, and conducting
aerial surveys to detect fires early (Figure 2). Once a fire is detected, suppression
efforts are swiftly initiated, involving rapid response, firefighting operations, and
coordination with emergency services. Finally, post-fire management is critical,
entailing damage assessment, rehabilitation, and restoration efforts, including
reforestation and habitat restoration, and ongoing monitoring and maintenance to
ensure long-term ecosystem health and resilience (Roy et al., 2010; NAPFF, 2018).
Important components of forest fire management are described hereunder.

Preventive measures
Silvicultural practices
To disrupt fuel continuity, effective silvicultural practices are necessary, including
weeding, cleaning, and removing dead vegetation. Thinning, planned grazing, and
promoting fire-resistant species can also help. Additionally, canopy treatments can
alter forest structure, reducing fuel sources and promoting regeneration. These
practices can enhance forest resilience and aid in recovery from fires (SFFMI, 2018;
NAPFF, 2018).
Controlled burning
Controlled burning, or prescribed burning, is a forest fire management technique
that involves intentionally setting fires in a controlled manner to achieve ecological
and management goals. Benefits include reducing fuel loads, promoting forest
regeneration, maintaining ecosystem balance, and enhancing biodiversity. However,
it requires favourable weather conditions, consideration of air quality, public
education, and a supportive regulatory framework (NAPFF, 2018).

Green fire belt
Constructing a green fire belt is an effective way to prevent forest fires. The green
fire belt is a fire-resistant zone created around the forest perimeter, typically 10-20
meters wide. To construct this belt, fire-tolerant trees are selected and planted in the
designated area. The soil is prepared by removing flammable materials and debris,
and a drip irrigation system is installed to maintain soil moisture. Regular
maintenance, including removing weeds, pruning plants, and inspecting for signs of
fire damage, is essential to ensure the green fire belt remains effective (NAPFF,
2013; Kumar, 2021a).
Awareness programs
An awareness program for forest fire targeted at local communities aims to educate
local communities about the risks and consequences of forest fires, promote
preventive measures and safe practices, encouraging community participation in
forest fire prevention and management. The program should employ various
awareness strategies, including community meetings, posters and pamphlets,
demonstrations, and community-led fire patrols. Key messages emphasize that
forest fires are preventable, the importance of immediate forest fire reports, and the
crucial role of community participation. Collaboration with local authorities and
community leaders is essential to the program's success, which is evaluated through
pre- and post-program surveys and community feedback (Kumar, 2021b).
Detection
Conventional methods of forest fire detection rely on routine inspections and
intelligence gathered from local communities and villagers. In contrast,
contemporary approaches leverage cutting-edge technologies (ISFR, 2015; ISFR,
2023; ISFR, 2021) including:
Satellite Imagery: Satellites such as IRS and INSAT provide high-resolution images
of forest areas facilitating fire detection.
Aerial Surveillance: Aircraft and helicopters are employed to monitor forest areas
and identify potential fires.
Wireless Sensor Networks: These networks of sensors detect and report changes in
forest conditions, enabling early fire detection.
Mobile Applications: Apps like Forest Fire Alert System (FFAS) and Van Vigyan
enable users to report forest fires and receive critical alerts.

Forest Fire Alert System: It is a vital tool in India's forest fire management strategy,
offering:
a) Near Real-Time Alerts: The system generates timely alerts based on fire
point data processed by the National Remote Sensing Centre.
b) MODIS Sensor Data: The system utilizes MODIS sensor data from Aqua
and Terra satellites to detect forest fires.
c) SMS Alerts: Users can register for SMS alerts, which are sent after
verification through a one-time password.
d) Dissemination to Local Authorities: The system ensures that forest fire
alerts are disseminated to local authorities and forest officials facilitating
prompt action.
e) Geographic Information System (GIS) and Global Positioning System
(GPS): These technologies enable precise location and tracking of forest
fires.
f) Remote Sensing: Hyperspectral and multispectral imaging technologies
detect and analyze forest fires.
g) Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML): AI and ML
algorithms analyze satellite images to identify and predict forest fires.
Suppression
Manual beating
Manual beating or smothering is a traditional method of suppressing forest fires,
particularly in areas where mechanized equipment cannot reach. This technique
involves using fire beaters, such as long-handled tools with a flat or curved blade,
to beat the fire and create a fire break. The beating pattern typically starts from the
outside and works its way inwards, targeting the base of the fire where the flames
are smallest. Alternatively, natural materials like dirt, sand, or green leaves can be
used to smother the fire by gently pressing them onto the flames (Maurya et al.,
2022).
Counter-fire
Counter-firing involves intentionally starting a new fire to combat an existing one,
with the goal of reducing the intensity and spread of a forest fire. However, the
effectiveness of this technique depends on various factors, including terrain, weather
conditions, and fuel types, which can affect the uniformity of the wildfire front and
ultimately determine the success of counter-firing (Murthy et al., 2006).

Fire lines
Fire line is a crucial tool in suppressing forest fires, and its strategic use can
effectively contain and extinguish fires. Creating a fire line involves clearing
vegetation and other combustible materials along the line, digging a trench, and
using fire-resistant materials to line the trench. They are also prepared through
cleared strips of vegetation that help contain or slow down fires. There are different
types of fire lines, including direct, indirect, and contingency fire lines, each with
its own specific purpose. Direct fire lines are created directly in front of the
advancing fire to stop its spread, while indirect fire lines are created parallel to the
fire to contain it and allow for backburning. Contingency fire lines are created in
anticipation of the fire's potential spread, providing a backup plan in case the
primary fire line is breached (SFFMI, 2018). Fire lines can also be internal fire lines
to prevent fire spread within a forest or external fire lines to prevent fires from
entering the forest. The benefits of fire lines include containment, extinguishing, and
reduced risk of the fire spreading. Fire lines typically range from 5-30 m in width,
depending on forest type and terrain. However, to minimize timber loss, external
and internal fire lines are often limited to 3 m and 1.5 m widths, respectively
(Nithyavathy et al., 2021).
Capacity building
Capacity building for the management of forest fires involves enhancing the skills,
knowledge, and abilities of individuals and organizations to prevent, detect, and
respond to forest fires effectively. This can be achieved through training programs
on firefighting techniques, fire prevention and detection, and emergency response
planning. Workshops and seminars on forest fire management, communication, and
coordination also play a crucial role (Sewak et al., 2021). Additionally, providing
access to modern firefighting equipment and technologies such as GIS and remote
sensing can enhance response efforts. Collaboration and partnerships among
government agencies, NGOs, local communities, and international organizations are
also essential. Furthermore, community engagement through public awareness
campaigns and community-based fire management initiatives can empower local
communities to take participation in forest fire prevention and response (Sagar et
al., 2024).
Community participation
Local communities residing in or near forests can play a vital role in combating
forest fires, as they are often the first responders and can provide crucial support in

extinguishing blazes. Community participation is crucial for the effective
management of forest fires (Sewak et al., 2021Sa). It raises awareness about the
risks and consequences of forest fires, encouraging individuals to take proactive
measures to prevent them. Community involvement fosters collaboration between
local residents, firefighters, and forest managers, ensuring a coordinated response to
forest fires. Local residents possess valuable knowledge about the forest, including
its terrain, vegetation, etc. which supports fire prevention efforts. Community
participation also enhances the response to forest fires, promotes sustainable forest
management practices, and empowers local communities to take ownership of forest
fire management, promoting a sense of responsibility and stewardship for the forest
(Satendra and Kaushik, 2014).
Surface clearing
Surface clearing is an effective method for suppressing forest fires, which involves
removing combustible materials such as dead leaves, twigs, and other vegetation
from the surface of the forest floor. This creates a barrier between the fire and
potential fuel sources, starving the fire of the oxygen and fuel it needs to spread. By
clearing the surface, firefighters can contain the fire and prevent it from jumping to
other areas, allowing them to gain control and eventually extinguish the blaze.
Surface clearing can be done using hand tools such as rakes, shovels, and axes, or
with mechanical equipment like bulldozers and tractors. This technique is
particularly useful in areas with dense undergrowth or where the fire is spreading
rapidly, as it helps to slow the fire's progress and give firefighters a chance to gain
the upper hand.
Dousing
Dousing is a crucial technique used to suppress forest fires, which involves applying
water or fire retardants directly to the fire to cool the fuel and reduce the intensity
of the flames. This method is effective in slowing the spread of the fire, allowing
firefighters to gain control and eventually extinguish the blaze. Dousing can be done
using various tools, such as water bombers, hoses, and buckets, and can be applied
directly to the fire or to the surrounding areas to create a fire break. The goal of
dousing is to reduce the temperature of the fuel to a point where it can no longer
sustain combustion, effectively starving the fire of the oxygen and heat it needs to
continue burning. By dousing the fire, firefighters can create a safe area for
themselves to work in and prevent the fire from spreading to other areas, ultimately
bringing the blaze under control.

Post fire Management Measures
Assessment, Rehabilitation and Recovery
Reporting and rehabilitation are crucial components of post-fire management,
playing a vital role in mitigating the impacts of forest fires and promoting
sustainable forest ecosystem recovery. Reporting helps to assess the extent of
damage caused by the fire, identify the causes of the fire, document lessons learned,
and inform rehabilitation efforts. Rehabilitation, on the other hand, involves
restoring degraded or damaged ecosystems, promoting biodiversity and ecosystem
resilience, and revegetation efforts to stabilize soils and prevent erosion.
Additionally, rehabilitation helps restore wildlife habitats, promoting the recovery
of affected species, and provides opportunities for community engagement,
education, and participation in forest restoration efforts. Overall, reporting and
rehabilitation enhance ecosystem resilience, improve fire management, provide
community benefits, and promote long-term sustainability, ensuring that forest
ecosystems are managed in a way that balances human needs with environmental
conservation.
Other Management strategies
National Policies
The Indian Forest Act, 1927, prohibits setting fire to forests, considering it a
criminal offense. Those found guilty may face imprisonment, fines, or both.
Exceptions include authorized burning for specific purposes and traditional
practices by indigenous communities.
The Wildlife Protection Act, 1972, prohibits the setting of fires in wildlife
sanctuaries and national parks, as it can cause harm to wildlife and their habitats.
Section 29 of the Act specifically prohibits the setting of fires, and violators can face
penalties, including imprisonment and fines.
The National Forest Fire Action Plan was published in 2013 with the aim of
preventing, detecting, and controlling forest fires in India. The plan, formulated by
the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change, Government of India,
addresses the growing concern of forest fires and their impact on the environment,
wildlife, and human lives. The plan focuses on prevention, detection, control, and
mitigation measures, including community engagement and education. Its
objectives include reducing forest fire incidents, protecting biodiversity, promoting
sustainable forest management, and enhancing community resilience.

State Policies for forest fire management
State policies for forest fire management in India vary from state to state, but most
states have their own policies and guidelines to prevent, detect, and control forest
fires. These policies focus on prevention, detection, control, and community
engagement, and include measures such as creating fire breaks, conducting
awareness campaigns, imposing penalties for reckless behavior, and using
technology such as satellite imagery and drones to detect fires early. States also
collaborate with neighbouring states, central government agencies, and NGOs to
share resources, expertise, and best practices. For example, Uttarakhand, Himachal
Pradesh, and Odisha have their own forest fire management policies that emphasize
prevention, detection, and control measures, including community engagement and
collaboration. These policies have helped reduce the number of forest fires and the
area affected by fires, improved community awareness and involvement, and
enhanced collaboration among different agencies, leading to more effective forest
fire management.
India has been struggling with the issue of forest fires, which pose a significant
threat to the country's biodiversity, ecosystem, and human settlements. To address
this challenge, the Indian government has implemented various forest fire
management strategies, including the National Forest Policy (1988), Forest Fire
Management Policy, and National Action Plan on Forest Fires. Additionally, many
states have developed their own plans to address forest fires. Technological
interventions, such as the Forest Fire Alert System (FFAS) and Forest Fire
Monitoring System, have also been employed to detect and track forest fires.
Furthermore, community-based initiatives, including Joint Forest Management
(JFM) and Eco-Development Committees, have been established to promote
sustainable forest management and engage local communities in forest fire
prevention and management. Despite these efforts, challenges persist, including
limited resources, climate change, and the need for increased community
engagement. The present study aims to congregate the models of forest fire
management strategies adopted by India in various states of the country.
To improve policy for the proper management of forest fires, a comprehensive
policy framework should be developed, clearly defining roles and responsibilities
among government agencies, local communities, and stakeholders. Policies should
support prescribed burning, invest in advanced fire detection systems, and establish
emergency response plans. Additionally, policies should address post-fire recovery

should be established to share knowledge and resources, and policies should be
regularly reviewed and revised to ensure effectiveness and relevance, engaging
stakeholders to reflect their needs and concerns.
