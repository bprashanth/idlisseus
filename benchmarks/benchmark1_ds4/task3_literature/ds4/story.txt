The morning light came gray and grudging through the fog, as if the sea itself were reluctant to begin another day. Elias stood at the top of the spiral stair, coffee cup warm against his palm, and watched the beam of the old Fresnel lens pulse once more into the brine. He had counted those pulses for forty years—through gales that shook the granite foundation, through summers so still the gulls seemed painted against the sky. Now the brass plaque on the wall told him what he already knew: Automated Beacon System, Installation Scheduled July. The words sat there, clean and official, as if they could erase a life.

He walked the circuit he had walked ten thousand times. He checked the oil level, the belt tension, the copper contacts he had polished smooth with his own thumb. In the engine room he laid a hand on the flywheel, felt the tremor that was the station's living pulse, and thought of the night his wife had visited for the last time, before the cancer took her. She had stood right here, shivering in his oilskin, and said, "You love this old thing more than me." He hadn't answered. He never answered. He only held her and watched the light sweep the black water.

This evening he climbed to the lantern room one final time before the crew arrived with the crates and the cutting torches. He sat on the worn wooden stool and looked out at the sea, which looked back at him with its ancient, unreadable face. He lit the lamp by hand, as he had always done, and let the beam fall across the water one last time. It was not the light that mattered, he understood now. It was the watching. The long, patient watching in the dark. He pulled the logbook from its shelf and wrote: *June 24. Wind northwest, moderate. Sea calm. Light extinguished at sunset.* Then he closed the cover, stood the book upright on the shelf, and turned off the lamp for the last time. The room went black. The sea did not notice.
