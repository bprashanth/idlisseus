On the morning the letter arrived, Elias climbed the spiral stairs as he had every dawn for forty years, his knees protesting the familiar ascent, his hands finding each worn rail without thought. The light still turned above him, patient and indifferent, throwing its white blade across a sea that had taken three ships and given back none of their names. He had learned to read the weather in the color of the horizon, to know by the pitch of the wind whether a fisherman would make it home, and to keep the lamp trimmed through nights when the fog came in like a living thing, pressing against the glass. The letter said the Coast Guard would install a sensor array in autumn. No keeper required. The lighthouse would blink on its own, correct and tireless, and the tower would stand empty except for the hum of machines.

He sat that evening on the catwalk with a cup of tea gone cold, watching the beam sweep the dark water in its old rhythm—three seconds on, seven off, a language he had spoken longer than he had spoken to any living person. Somewhere below, the village lights winked like distant answers to questions he had stopped asking. Elias thought of the winter of '87, when he had stayed awake six nights straight while the generator coughed and died and coughed again, keeping a hand lantern burning in the window so a trawler caught in the ice could find the channel. He thought of the girl who had climbed the stairs once, years ago, to see the lamp up close, her face gold in the Fresnel lens. None of that would be in the manual. None of it would matter to the sensor that would measure wind speed and adjust the beam and report faults to a desk three hundred miles inland.

When the sun rose, he polished the brass one last time—not because anyone would notice, but because the work had always been the point, the tending, the proof that someone was here, awake, accountable to the dark. The light turned. The sea breathed. Elias wound the clockwork mechanism with steady hands and listened to its heartbeat, knowing that soon there would be no heartbeat at all, only electricity and silence, and the coast would be guarded by something that never missed a night and never loved it either.
